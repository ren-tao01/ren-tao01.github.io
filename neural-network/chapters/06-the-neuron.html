The Neuron

One artificial neuron is a tiny machine: it takes a list of numbers, does a weighted sum, adds an offset, and squashes the result through a nonlinearity. Today we build it, and we examine each nonlinearity in turn.

The biological inspiration (briefly)

Real neurons are messy electrochemical machines. They have many input channels (dendrites), they integrate incoming signals, and they fire (along an axon) once the integrated signal crosses a threshold. The artificial neuron borrows the silhouette of this story — many inputs, weighted summing, a thresholdy nonlinearity — and almost none of the underlying biology. We do not need biology to understand what follows.

Anatomy of an artificial neuron

Inputs $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$. Weights $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n)$. A scalar bias $b$. An activation function $\sigma$. The neuron computes:

$$ z \;=\; \mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\, x_i + b, $$ $$ a \;=\; \sigma(z). $$

The number $z$ is the pre-activation; $a$ is the activation. The pre-activation is a dot product (Chapter 2) plus an offset; the activation is the same $z$ pushed through a nonlinear function.

Compare this to a single straight line $y = mx + b$. For one input it's identical, except instead of returning $z$ we return $\sigma(z)$. The activation function is what distinguishes a neural network from linear regression.

Why the nonlinearity matters

Suppose you stack two purely linear neurons: $a = w_2 (w_1 x + b_1) + b_2 = (w_1 w_2) x + (w_2 b_1 + b_2)$. That is a single line! Composition of linear maps is linear. So a stack of arbitrarily many linear layers can only ever express a single linear function. To represent richer relationships — curves, kinks, decision boundaries that aren't hyperplanes — you need a nonlinear step in between. We will prove the XOR example formally in Chapter 8.

Activation: sigmoid

$$ \sigma(z) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}. $$

The sigmoid takes any real input and squashes it into $(0, 1)$. That made it natural for binary classification, where the output should be interpretable as a probability. We computed its derivative in Chapter 3:

$$ \sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr). $$

Limitations:

  • Saturates: for large $|z|$ the slope is nearly zero, so gradients vanish — a major training difficulty for deep networks (Chapter 12).
  • Not zero-centered: outputs are always positive, which is mildly inconvenient for downstream layers.

Activation: tanh

$$ \tanh(z) \;=\; \frac{e^z - e^{-z}}{e^z + e^{-z}}. $$

Range $(-1, 1)$. Effectively a re-centered, re-scaled sigmoid: $\tanh(z) = 2\sigma(2z) - 1$. Its derivative is

$$ \frac{d}{dz} \tanh(z) = 1 - \tanh^2(z). $$

Strictly preferable to sigmoid in hidden layers (it is zero-centered) but still saturates at the extremes.

Activation: ReLU

$$ \mathrm{ReLU}(z) \;=\; \max(0, z). $$

The "rectified linear unit." Cheap to compute. Doesn't saturate for positive inputs. Its derivative is the simplest of the lot:

$$ \frac{d}{dz} \mathrm{ReLU}(z) = \begin{cases} 1 & z > 0 \\ 0 & z < 0 \end{cases} \quad\text{(at $z=0$, conventionally taken to be 0).} $$

ReLU is the default activation in most modern feed-forward layers. It has its own pathology — neurons with permanently-negative pre-activations stop learning ("dying ReLUs") — and several variants (Leaky ReLU, GELU, SiLU) try to soften the kink at zero. For our purposes, plain ReLU is the right starting point.

Activation: softmax (for the output layer)

When a network must output a probability over $C$ classes, we use the softmax. Given a vector $\mathbf{z} = (z_1, \ldots, z_C)$, define

$$ \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_k \;=\; \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{C} e^{z_j}}. $$

Properties:

  • Every output is in $(0, 1)$.
  • The outputs sum to 1 — they form a probability distribution.
  • It preserves the rank order of inputs (largest $z$ gives largest probability).
  • It is shift-invariant: subtracting the same constant from every $z$ does not change the result. We use this for numerical stability.

Softmax is the multi-class generalization of the sigmoid — and indeed, for two classes, softmax and sigmoid are equivalent.

The neuron in vector form

A whole layer of $m$ neurons receiving the same input vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ stacks into:

$$ \mathbf{z} \;=\; \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}, \qquad \mathbf{a} \;=\; \sigma(\mathbf{z}), $$

where $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is the weight matrix (each row is one neuron's weights), $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the bias vector, and $\sigma$ is applied element-wise. This is the entire computation of a fully-connected layer. Stack a few of these in a row and you have a multilayer perceptron — Chapter 8.

Counting parameters

A layer mapping $n$ inputs to $m$ outputs has $m \cdot n$ weights and $m$ biases — a total of $m(n+1)$ trainable numbers. A network with three layers of widths $784 \to 256 \to 64 \to 10$ (a small MNIST classifier) therefore has

$$ 256 \cdot 785 + 64 \cdot 257 + 10 \cdot 65 \;=\; 200\,960 + 16\,448 + 650 \;=\; 218\,058 $$

parameters. The right scale to think about. Modern language models simply have more zeros.

Check yourself

  1. Compute one neuron's output. Inputs $(1, 2, 3)$, weights $(0.5, -1, 2)$, bias $0$, activation ReLU. What is the output?
  2. Why is purely linear stacking equivalent to one linear layer?
  3. Why does softmax need division by a sum?
  4. What is $\sigma'(z)$ at the value of $z$ where $\sigma(z) = 0.9$?
  5. How many trainable parameters in a layer mapping $1024 \to 1024$?

Answers: $z = 0.5 - 2 + 6 = 4.5$, ReLU keeps it; output $4.5$. Composition of linear maps is linear. Division ensures outputs sum to 1. $0.9 \cdot 0.1 = 0.09$. $1024 \cdot 1024 + 1024 = 1\,049\,600$.
