Loss & Gradient Descent

How wrong are we? How do we get less wrong? Two losses (squared error and cross-entropy), one update rule, and a numerical example you can do by hand.

What a loss function is

A loss function is a single number that measures how badly a model's prediction differs from the truth. Smaller is better, with $0$ meaning "perfect on this example." Once we have a loss, training reduces to a single goal: minimize the average loss across the training data by adjusting the parameters $\mathbf{w}$.

Two losses cover almost everything we will do in this site:

  • Mean squared error for regression (continuous outputs).
  • Cross-entropy for classification (probabilities over discrete classes).

Mean squared error

Given predictions $\hat{y}_i$ and true values $y_i$ for $N$ examples, the mean squared error is

$$ L_{\mathrm{MSE}} \;=\; \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2. $$

Why squared, not absolute value? Three reasons:

  • It is differentiable everywhere (no kink at zero).
  • It penalizes large errors disproportionately, which often matches our intuition: missing by $10$ is much worse than missing by $1$.
  • Under the assumption of Gaussian noise around the true value, minimizing MSE is exactly maximum likelihood. (We will not use this fact directly; it is reassuring that the loss is principled.)

The factor $\tfrac{1}{N}$ keeps the loss comparable across dataset sizes. Many texts also include $\tfrac{1}{2}$ in front so that derivatives come out tidy; that is just a convention.

Cross-entropy loss

For a single classification example with $C$ classes, true label $y \in \{1,\ldots,C\}$, and predicted probabilities $\hat{y}_1, \ldots, \hat{y}_C$,

$$ L_{\mathrm{CE}} \;=\; -\ln \hat{y}_y \;=\; -\sum_{k=1}^{C} \mathbb{1}[k = y]\, \ln \hat{y}_k. $$

That second form (with the indicator $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$) is what you see written when $y$ is a one-hot vector. We unpacked the meaning in Chapter 5: cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood of the correct class. Probability $1$ on the correct class gives loss $0$; probability close to $0$ gives loss exploding to $+\infty$.

For a whole batch of $N$ examples, average:

$$ L_{\mathrm{CE}} \;=\; -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \ln \hat{y}_{i, y_i}. $$

The loss surface

Imagine plotting $L(\mathbf{w})$ — the loss as a function of all parameters. With one parameter it's a curve. With two it's a surface — hills and valleys above the parameter plane. With millions, your mental picture has to remain abstract, but the words still work: there are valleys (low loss) and ridges (high loss), and we want to slide downhill toward a valley floor.

The update rule

From Chapter 4 we know $\nabla L(\mathbf{w})$ points uphill. We want downhill, so we step in the opposite direction:

$$ \boxed{\;\mathbf{w}_{\text{new}} \;=\; \mathbf{w}_{\text{old}} \;-\; \eta\, \nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{\text{old}}).\;} $$

That's it. That is the entire learning algorithm. Everything else in the next dozen chapters is a way to compute the gradient on the right efficiently, or a way to make the step on the left more reliable.

$\eta > 0$ is the learning rate. Too small and learning crawls. Too large and you overshoot the valley and bounce around — or diverge entirely. Chapter 10 discusses how to choose and adapt it.

A worked one-dimensional example

Let's minimize $L(w) = (w - 3)^2$ by hand. The optimal answer is obviously $w = 3$ (the loss is zero there). We pretend we don't know that and let gradient descent find it.

Compute the gradient (just an ordinary derivative here): $L'(w) = 2(w - 3)$. Pick $\eta = 0.1$ and start at $w_0 = 0$.

A learning-rate cautionary tale

Try the same example with $\eta = 1.1$. The update becomes $w \to w - 2.2(w-3) = -1.2w + 6.6$. Equivalently, $(w_\text{new} - 3) = -1.2 (w - 3)$ — the distance to the minimum is multiplied by $-1.2$ at every step, so it grows.

Concretely, starting at $w_0 = 0$:

  • $w_1 = -1.2(0) + 6.6 = 6.6$,   $L = 12.96$.
  • $w_2 = -1.2(6.6) + 6.6 = -1.32$,   $L \approx 18.66$.
  • $w_3 = -1.2(-1.32) + 6.6 = 8.184$,   $L \approx 26.87$.

Each step ricochets to the opposite side of the minimum and lands further away. The loss grows by a factor of $1.44$ per step. The right learning rate matters.

Batch, stochastic, and mini-batch gradient descent

Real datasets have millions of examples. Computing the exact gradient $\nabla L$ over all of them at every step is wasteful. Three flavors:

  • Batch GD: use the entire dataset per step. Accurate gradient, very slow.
  • Stochastic GD (SGD): use one example per step. Cheap and noisy. Good early; oscillates near the minimum.
  • Mini-batch SGD: use a small batch (32 to 512 examples) per step. Best of both worlds; this is what everyone uses in practice.

We will keep saying "gradient descent" and assume mini-batch unless noted.

Local versus global minima

Gradient descent only goes downhill. If the loss surface has multiple valleys, GD will find a valley near where you started — not necessarily the deepest one. For convex losses (a single bowl) this distinction does not matter. For deep neural networks it does, in principle, but practical loss surfaces have a remarkable property: many local minima are roughly as good as the best one. We won't prove that. Just be aware that GD's guarantee is "local," and that this is fine in practice.
