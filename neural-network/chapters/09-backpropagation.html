Backpropagation

The chain rule, applied carefully, in reverse. We derive the four backprop equations and trace one full numerical example through a 2-2-1 network.

The setup

Recall the forward pass from Chapter 8:

$$ \mathbf{z}^{(\ell)} = \mathbf{W}^{(\ell)} \mathbf{a}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}, \qquad \mathbf{a}^{(\ell)} = \sigma^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}). $$

Suppose the loss is some scalar $L$ that depends on the final activation $\mathbf{a}^{(L)}$ and the true label. To do gradient descent (Chapter 7) we need the gradient of $L$ with respect to every parameter in every layer: $\partial L / \partial \mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}$ and $\partial L / \partial \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}$.

Backpropagation is an algorithm to compute all of these efficiently in a single backward sweep through the network. It's the multivariable chain rule (Chapter 4), organized so we never repeat work.

Why backwards, not forwards

We could, in principle, compute partial derivatives by perturbing each parameter and re-running the forward pass — that's numerical differentiation. With $P$ parameters and $F$ forward FLOPs, this costs roughly $P \cdot F$. For modern networks $P$ is in the billions; that's a non-starter.

A smarter idea: apply the chain rule once, all the way through. The chain rule lets you decompose $\partial L / \partial w$ into a product of derivatives along the path from $w$ to $L$. If we organize the computation properly, we can compute all gradients in time comparable to a single forward pass. The trick is to start at $L$ and work backwards.

One sentence summary

Forward pass computes activations from input to output. Backward pass propagates "error signals" from the loss back to every parameter, multiplying by Jacobians along the way.

The error signal $\boldsymbol{\delta}$

Define, for each layer $\ell$, the error signal

$$ \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)} \;=\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}}. $$

That is: how does the loss change if I were to wiggle the pre-activations of layer $\ell$? It's a vector with the same shape as $\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}$. Once we know all the $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)}$'s, the gradients with respect to weights and biases drop out almost for free.

The four backprop equations

Equation 1 — error at the output layer

At the last layer, $\mathbf{a}^{(L)}$ is the output and $L$ is the loss. By the chain rule,

$$ \delta_i^{(L)} \;=\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i^{(L)}} \;=\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_i^{(L)}} \cdot \sigma'^{(L)}\bigl(z_i^{(L)}\bigr). $$

That is, the gradient of the loss with respect to the output activation, times the derivative of the output's nonlinearity at the corresponding pre-activation.

Equation 2 — propagate the error backward

The error at layer $\ell$ depends on the error at layer $\ell+1$ pulled back through the linear map plus the local nonlinearity:

$$ \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)} \;=\; \bigl(\mathbf{W}^{(\ell+1)}\bigr)^{\!\top}\, \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell+1)} \;\odot\; \sigma'^{(\ell)}\bigl(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}\bigr). $$

$\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. Read it: "take next layer's error, push it back through the transposed weight matrix, then gate by the local activation derivative."

Equation 3 — gradient with respect to biases

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}} \;=\; \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)}. $$

That's it. The bias gradient at layer $\ell$ is just the error signal at layer $\ell$. (Why: $\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)} = \cdots + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}$, so $\partial \mathbf{z}^{(\ell)} / \partial \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}$ is the identity matrix.)

Equation 4 — gradient with respect to weights

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}} \;=\; \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)}\, \bigl(\mathbf{a}^{(\ell-1)}\bigr)^{\!\top}. $$

Outer product: error at this layer (column) times the previous layer's activation (row). The result has shape $n_\ell \times n_{\ell-1}$ — exactly the shape of $\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}$.

Memorize these four. With them, training a deep network is just: run the forward pass, run the backward pass, update parameters, repeat.

Derivation of the four equations

We derive Equation 2 (the others follow easily). Pick a hidden layer $\ell$ and one of its components $z_i^{(\ell)}$. The path from $z_i^{(\ell)}$ to the loss goes through layer $\ell+1$. So by the multivariable chain rule,

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i^{(\ell)}} \;=\; \sum_k \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_k^{(\ell+1)}} \cdot \frac{\partial z_k^{(\ell+1)}}{\partial z_i^{(\ell)}}. $$

The first factor is $\delta_k^{(\ell+1)}$. For the second, recall $z_k^{(\ell+1)} = \sum_j W_{kj}^{(\ell+1)} a_j^{(\ell)} + b_k^{(\ell+1)}$ and $a_j^{(\ell)} = \sigma(z_j^{(\ell)})$. So

$$ \frac{\partial z_k^{(\ell+1)}}{\partial z_i^{(\ell)}} \;=\; \sum_j W_{kj}^{(\ell+1)} \cdot \frac{\partial a_j^{(\ell)}}{\partial z_i^{(\ell)}} \;=\; W_{ki}^{(\ell+1)} \cdot \sigma'(z_i^{(\ell)}). $$

(Only $j = i$ survives the partial.) Plug back in:

$$ \delta_i^{(\ell)} \;=\; \sum_k W_{ki}^{(\ell+1)}\, \delta_k^{(\ell+1)} \cdot \sigma'(z_i^{(\ell)}) \;=\; \bigl[(\mathbf{W}^{(\ell+1)})^\top \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell+1)}\bigr]_i \cdot \sigma'(z_i^{(\ell)}). $$

That is Equation 2. Equation 1 is the same idea at the boundary, where there is no further layer above. Equations 3 and 4 follow by writing out $\partial L / \partial b_i^{(\ell)}$ and $\partial L / \partial W_{ij}^{(\ell)}$ and noticing each of them is a single chain step.

A full worked example

We use the 2-2-1 network from Chapter 8 with ReLU hidden activation and identity output, trained against MSE on a single example. Recap of the forward pass with input $\mathbf{x} = (2, 1)$ and target $y = 1$:

$$ \mathbf{W}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0.5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{W}^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b^{(2)} = 0.5. $$

Forward pass:

$$ \mathbf{z}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix},\ \mathbf{a}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix},\quad z^{(2)} = -2.5,\ \hat{y} = -2.5. $$

Loss $L = \tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{y} - y)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(-2.5 - 1)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(12.25) = 6.125$. The factor of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ makes the derivative tidy.

Step 1 — error at the output

$L = \tfrac12(\hat y - y)^2$, so $\partial L / \partial \hat{y} = \hat{y} - y = -3.5$. The output activation is the identity, so its derivative is $1$, and

$$ \delta^{(2)} \;=\; -3.5. $$

Step 2 — gradient of layer-2 weights and bias

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(2)}} = \delta^{(2)} \cdot (\mathbf{a}^{(1)})^\top = -3.5 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3.5 & -7.0 \end{bmatrix}. $$ $$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial b^{(2)}} = \delta^{(2)} = -3.5. $$

Step 3 — propagate error to layer 1

First the linear pull-back: $(\mathbf{W}^{(2)})^\top \delta^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \cdot (-3.5) = \begin{bmatrix} -3.5 \\ 7.0 \end{bmatrix}.$

Now the local activation derivative. ReLU's derivative is $1$ where the pre-activation is positive, $0$ where it is negative. Both $z_1^{(1)} = 1$ and $z_2^{(1)} = 2$ are positive, so $\sigma'(\mathbf{z}^{(1)}) = (1, 1)$.

$$ \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} -3.5 \\ 7.0 \end{bmatrix} \odot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3.5 \\ 7.0 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Step 4 — gradient of layer-1 weights and bias

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(1)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} (\mathbf{x})^\top = \begin{bmatrix} -3.5 \\ 7.0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -7.0 & -3.5 \\ 14.0 & 7.0 \end{bmatrix}. $$ $$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(1)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} -3.5 \\ 7.0 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Step 5 — one gradient descent step

Pick $\eta = 0.01$. Update each parameter by subtracting $\eta$ times its gradient:

$$ \mathbf{W}^{(1)}_\text{new} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0.5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} - 0.01 \begin{bmatrix} -7.0 & -3.5 \\ 14.0 & 7.0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.07 & -0.965 \\ 0.36 & 1.93 \end{bmatrix}. $$ $$ \mathbf{b}^{(1)}_\text{new} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} - 0.01 \begin{bmatrix} -3.5 \\ 7.0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.035 \\ -1.07 \end{bmatrix}. $$ $$ \mathbf{W}^{(2)}_\text{new} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} - 0.01 \begin{bmatrix} -3.5 & -7.0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.035 & -1.93 \end{bmatrix}. $$ $$ b^{(2)}_\text{new} = 0.5 - 0.01 (-3.5) = 0.535. $$

Sanity check — the loss should drop

Re-run the forward pass with the new parameters and the same input $\mathbf{x} = (2, 1)$:

$$ \mathbf{z}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.07 \cdot 2 + (-0.965) \cdot 1 \\ 0.36 \cdot 2 + 1.93 \cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0.035 \\ -1.07 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.21 \\ 1.58 \end{bmatrix}. $$ $$ \mathbf{a}^{(1)} = (1.21,\ 1.58), \qquad z^{(2)} = 1.035 \cdot 1.21 + (-1.93) \cdot 1.58 + 0.535 \approx -1.262. $$ $$ \hat{y} = -1.262, \quad L = \tfrac12 (-1.262 - 1)^2 \approx 2.56. $$

Loss dropped from $6.125$ to $2.56$ in one gradient step. The procedure works.

The picture: forward and backward as paired sweeps

Softmax + cross-entropy: a beautifully simple gradient

The classification head uses softmax outputs and cross-entropy loss. If $y$ is the one-hot true label and $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is the predicted probability vector, the gradient of the cross-entropy with respect to the pre-softmax pre-activations $\mathbf{z}^{(L)}$ collapses spectacularly:

$$ \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)} \;=\; \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}. $$

Just the prediction minus the truth. No log, no exponential, no Jacobian. This identity is the reason "softmax + cross-entropy" became the default classification head — it gives backpropagation a clean place to start.

The proof is two lines of algebra: write $\hat{y}_k = e^{z_k}/\sum_j e^{z_j}$ and $L = -\sum_k y_k \ln \hat{y}_k$, then differentiate. Most of the messy terms cancel.

Check yourself

  1. Why does the weight gradient $\partial L / \partial \mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}$ have the same shape as $\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}$?
  2. Why does propagating error backward use $\mathbf{W}^{(\ell+1)\top}$ instead of $\mathbf{W}^{(\ell+1)}$?
  3. If the activation derivative is zero at some neuron's pre-activation, what happens to that neuron's gradient signal?
  4. Why is "softmax + cross-entropy" a uniquely tidy combination?
  5. Numerical-differentiation cost vs. backprop cost — what's the comparison?

Answers: outer product of two column vectors with shapes $n_\ell$ and $n_{\ell-1}$ gives an $n_\ell \times n_{\ell-1}$ matrix. The forward pass uses $\mathbf{W}$ to map activations forward; the backward pass uses $\mathbf{W}^\top$ because differentiating a matrix-vector product flips the dimensions. The signal is multiplied by zero — the neuron is "dead" for that step and learns nothing. The exponential in softmax cancels the logarithm in cross-entropy, leaving $\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}$. Numerical differentiation needs $\mathcal{O}(P)$ forward passes; backprop needs about one. Asymptotic difference is enormous.
