The Multilayer Perceptron — Forward Pass

Stack the neuron from Chapter 6 into layers. Pass an input through them in matrix form. Then prove that without nonlinearities the whole tower would collapse to a single line.

Layers, in symbols

Take an input $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_0}$ and hide $L$ layers between input and output. Each layer $\ell$ has its own weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}$, bias $\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}$, and activation $\sigma^{(\ell)}$. Define $\mathbf{a}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x}$, then for $\ell = 1, 2, \ldots, L$:

$$ \mathbf{z}^{(\ell)} = \mathbf{W}^{(\ell)} \mathbf{a}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}, \qquad \mathbf{a}^{(\ell)} = \sigma^{(\ell)}\bigl(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}\bigr). $$

The last activation $\mathbf{a}^{(L)}$ is the network's output. For classification we typically take $\sigma^{(L)} = \mathrm{softmax}$; for regression we usually take $\sigma^{(L)} = \mathrm{identity}$.

Shapes:

  • $\mathbf{a}^{(\ell-1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell-1}}$.
  • $\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell \times n_{\ell-1}}$.
  • $\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}, \mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}, \mathbf{a}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell}$.

Picture of an MLP

For this little network the three weight matrices have shapes $4 \times 3$, $2 \times 4$, and $1 \times 2$, plus biases of length $4$, $2$, and $1$ respectively. Total parameters: $12 + 8 + 2 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 29$.

The forward pass, step by step

Computing the network's output for an input $\mathbf{x}$ is called the forward pass. It is just plugging $\mathbf{x}$ through the layered formula:

  1. $\mathbf{a}^{(0)} \leftarrow \mathbf{x}$.
  2. For $\ell = 1, \ldots, L$: compute $\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)} = \mathbf{W}^{(\ell)} \mathbf{a}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}$, then $\mathbf{a}^{(\ell)} = \sigma^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)})$.
  3. Return $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{a}^{(L)}$.

That's the whole inference algorithm. Multiplications and additions with a few elementwise nonlinearities sprinkled in.

A small numerical forward pass

Take a $2 \to 2 \to 1$ network with ReLU hidden activation and identity output. Suppose:

$$ \mathbf{W}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0.5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{W}^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b^{(2)} = 0.5. $$

Input $\mathbf{x} = (2, 1)$.

Layer 1:

$$ \mathbf{z}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0.5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\!\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{a}^{(1)} = \mathrm{ReLU}(\mathbf{z}^{(1)}) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Layer 2:

$$ z^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\!\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 0.5 = -2.5, \qquad \hat{y} = -2.5. $$

We will reuse this network in Chapter 9 as the working example for backpropagation.

Vectorized over a batch

In practice we run the forward pass on a whole batch of $N$ examples at once. Stack the inputs as columns of a matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_0 \times N}$, and every formula stays the same:

$$ \mathbf{Z}^{(\ell)} = \mathbf{W}^{(\ell)} \mathbf{A}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}, \qquad \mathbf{A}^{(\ell)} = \sigma^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{Z}^{(\ell)}). $$

The bias is added column-wise (broadcast). On a GPU this batched form runs at full hardware speed; that is why mini-batch training is fast.

Why we need nonlinearities (the XOR proof)

Suppose every $\sigma^{(\ell)}$ is the identity. Then $\mathbf{a}^{(\ell)} = \mathbf{W}^{(\ell)} \mathbf{a}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}$, and a tower of layers expands as

$$ \mathbf{a}^{(L)} = \mathbf{W}^{(L)} \cdots \mathbf{W}^{(1)} \mathbf{x} + \text{(some constant)}. $$

The product of matrices is itself a matrix. So the whole network collapses to a single affine function of $\mathbf{x}$. Depth gives you nothing. The nonlinearities are what unlock representational power.

The XOR illustration

Consider four inputs and labels: $(0,0) \to 0$, $(0,1) \to 1$, $(1,0) \to 1$, $(1,1) \to 0$ — the XOR function. There is no straight line in the plane that puts the two $0$s on one side and the two $1$s on the other. So a one-layer linear classifier — which makes its decision based on whether $\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b$ is above zero — cannot solve XOR. Period.

Add one ReLU hidden layer with two units and you can solve it:

$$ \mathbf{W}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b}^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{w}^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b^{(2)} = 0. $$

Plug in each of the four inputs:

$\mathbf{x}$$\mathbf{z}^{(1)}$$\mathbf{a}^{(1)} = \mathrm{ReLU}(\mathbf{z}^{(1)})$$z^{(2)}$outputtarget
(0,0)(0, -1)(0, 0)000
(0,1)(1, 0)(1, 0)111
(1,0)(1, 0)(1, 0)111
(1,1)(2, 1)(2, 1)000

Two hidden ReLU units, four free parameters of weights and two of biases, and the network gets XOR exactly right. The kink at zero is doing the work.

Universal approximation, briefly

A theorem due to Cybenko (1989), Hornik (1991), and others states roughly: a single hidden layer of finite width with a non-trivial activation function can approximate any continuous function on a bounded domain to any desired accuracy. So MLPs are universal — given enough neurons.

Two cautions:

  • "Enough neurons" can mean astronomically many. In practice, going deeper rather than wider lets you express the same function with vastly fewer parameters.
  • Universality says nothing about whether gradient descent will find the right weights from a random start. Optimization is a separate problem.

Check yourself

  1. If a network has architecture $784 \to 256 \to 256 \to 10$ with biases everywhere, how many parameters?
  2. Why does a stack of identity-activation layers collapse to one linear map?
  3. Why is the bias added column-wise when the inputs are stacked as columns?
  4. Run the small numerical example with $\mathbf{x} = (1, 1)$ and the same weights. What is $\hat{y}$?
  5. Universal approximation says you can approximate any continuous function with one hidden layer. Why do we use deeper networks anyway?

Answers: $256\cdot785 + 256\cdot257 + 10\cdot257 = 200{,}960 + 65{,}792 + 2{,}570 = 269{,}322$. Composition of linear maps is linear. The bias should add the same amount to every example in the batch. $\mathbf{z}^{(1)} = (0, 1.5)$, ReLU gives $(0, 1.5)$, $z^{(2)} = -3 + 0.5 = -2.5$, $\hat{y} = -2.5$. Depth gives much more parameter efficiency in practice and learns hierarchical features.
