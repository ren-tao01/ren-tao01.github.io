Multivariable & Gradients

Functions that depend on many things at once. Partial derivatives. The gradient — a compass that always points uphill. And gradient descent, the rule that lets a neural network teach itself.

Why we need this

In Chapter 3 we met the derivative: a single number telling us the slope of a function at a point. It answered the question, "if I nudge the input a tiny bit, how does the output change?" That was enough when the function had only one input. But neural networks don't.

A real network has thousands, millions, sometimes billions of trainable weights. Each weight is just a number we are allowed to adjust. The error of the network — how wrong its predictions are — depends on every single one of them simultaneously. Turn one weight up a little; the error shifts. Turn a different weight down a little; the error shifts in a completely different way. We need calculus that can keep track of all of these influences at once.

Picture this

Imagine a giant control panel covered in dials. Each dial is one weight in the network. There's a single big display that reads "Error: some number." Your job is to turn the dials so the error gets as small as possible. Some dials matter a lot. Some barely move the needle. Multivariable calculus is the math that tells you which dial to turn, and by how much.

A tiny everyday example

Forget neural networks for a moment. Suppose your exam score depends on two things: how many hours you studied ($s$) and how many hours you slept the night before ($z$). You might guess at a rough relationship like

$$ \text{Score}(s, z) \;=\; 5\,s \;+\; 2\,z. $$

This is a function of two variables. Doing well requires both. Studying without sleep helps a little; sleeping without studying helps a little; doing both helps a lot. If you wanted to push your score up by one point as cheaply as possible, you'd want to know: which one — an extra hour of study or an extra hour of sleep — moves the score more? That is exactly the question multivariable calculus answers, and it's the same question a neural network is asking, just with millions of variables instead of two.

Key idea

Each weight is like a knob you can turn. The network's error depends on all the knobs together. To train the network, we need a way to ask, for every knob, "if I turn just this one, does the error go up or down, and by how much?"

The training pipeline at a glance

Here is the loop we are slowly building toward. Don't memorize it — just glance at it and know it's coming:

prediction → error → loss → gradient → weight update → better prediction → …

This chapter lives in the middle. Once we have a single number called the loss that measures how wrong the network is, the gradient tells us how to change the weights to drive that number down. Everything else in the book builds on this one idea.

Partial derivatives

Suppose you're tasting a soup. It's a little off. Did you add too much salt? Too much pepper? Too much water? You can't easily tell from one taste. So what do you do? You change one ingredient at a time and taste again. If you adjust only the salt and the soup gets better, you've learned something about salt — the other ingredients didn't change, so they can't be responsible for the difference.

That is exactly what a partial derivative does. It freezes every variable except one and asks: if only this one variable changes, how fast does the function change?

Hill-walking analogy

You're standing on a hillside. The height of the ground depends on two coordinates: how far north you are, and how far east. Take one step strictly east (refusing to budge north or south) — the ground rises or falls by some amount. That rise-over-run is the partial derivative with respect to east. Take a step strictly north, and you measure the partial derivative with respect to north. Two different directions, two different slopes — both true at the same point.

Computing one

Let's try the function

$$ f(x, y) \;=\; x^2 + 3xy + y^3. $$

To compute the partial derivative with respect to $x$, we pretend $y$ is a fixed constant — a number that doesn't move — and differentiate the rest using the rules from Chapter 3:

  • $x^2$ differentiates to $2x$.
  • $3xy$ — because $y$ is being treated as a constant — is just $(3y) \cdot x$, which differentiates to $3y$.
  • $y^3$ has no $x$ in it at all, so its derivative with respect to $x$ is $0$.

Add them up:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \;=\; 2x + 3y. $$

Now switch roles: treat $x$ as the constant and differentiate with respect to $y$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \;=\; 3x + 3y^2. $$

Reading the notation

That curly $\partial$ symbol is read "partial." It looks like an italic $d$ with a flourish. The flourish is a small reminder: "there are other variables here, but I'm holding them still on purpose."

SymbolPlain English
$f(x, y)$A function whose value depends on both $x$ and $y$.
$\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$How fast $f$ changes when only $x$ moves and $y$ is held still.
$\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}$How fast $f$ changes when only $y$ moves and $x$ is held still.
$\dfrac{df}{dx}$ (no curl)The single-variable derivative from Chapter 3 — used when there's only one input.

Plugging in numbers

A formula like $\partial f / \partial x = 2x + 3y$ is still a function. To get an actual slope, plug in a point. At $(x, y) = (1, 2)$:

$$ \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{(1,2)} \!\!= 2(1) + 3(2) = 8, \qquad \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right|_{(1,2)} \!\!= 3(1) + 3(2)^2 = 15. $$

Read these out loud. At this specific point, nudging $x$ a tiny amount changes $f$ about 8 times as fast as the nudge; nudging $y$ by the same tiny amount changes $f$ about 15 times as fast. So $y$ has more leverage here than $x$. Different points in the plane will report different leverage — that's the whole point of doing calculus rather than algebra.

Key idea

A partial derivative is just an ordinary derivative — but you've sworn an oath to leave every other variable untouched while you compute it.

The gradient: a compass for functions

Single-variable derivatives have it easy. There's only one direction to move (left or right), so there's only one slope to talk about. Multivariable functions are different: from any given point you can move in infinitely many directions — northeast, due south, up-and-to-the-left, anything in between. Each direction has its own slope. Which one should we report?

The clean answer turns out to be: collect the partial derivatives — one per axis — into a single vector. That bundle has a name.

Compass analogy

Think of the gradient as a tiny compass placed at a point on a hillside. Instead of pointing north, the needle always points uphill in the steepest direction available. The longer the needle, the steeper the slope. At a flat spot — the bottom of a valley, the top of a peak, or a saddle — the needle has zero length and stops pointing anywhere.

Definition

For a function $f(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$, the gradient is the column vector that stacks together every partial derivative:

$$ \nabla f \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \partial f / \partial x_1 \\ \partial f / \partial x_2 \\ \vdots \\ \partial f / \partial x_n \end{bmatrix}. $$

That upside-down triangle, $\nabla$, is called nabla. You can read $\nabla f$ as "the gradient of $f$" or, in plain words, "all the partial derivatives of $f$, gathered into one list."

SymbolPlain English
$x_i$The $i$-th input variable (e.g. the $i$-th weight of the network).
$\partial f / \partial x_i$How fast $f$ changes when only $x_i$ moves.
$\nabla f$All those rates, packed into a single vector — one slot per input.
$\nabla f$ at point $\mathbf{p}$The compass needle at the location $\mathbf{p}$ in input space.

Two facts that make it useful

  1. The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase. Out of all directions you could step, this is the one that makes $f$ grow fastest.
  2. Its length tells you how steep that fastest direction is. A small gradient means a gentle slope; a large gradient means a cliff.

And here's the consequence we will lean on for the rest of the book:

Key idea

If $\nabla f$ points uphill, then its negative, $-\nabla f$, points downhill — and it's the steepest downhill direction available. To make $f$ smaller as fast as possible, take a small step in the direction of $-\nabla f$. Hold on to this sentence; it's the heart of how networks learn.

A concrete example: $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$

This function looks like a perfectly round bowl, lowest at the origin and rising in every direction. Let's compute its gradient.

Partial with respect to $x$ (treating $y$ as a constant): $\partial f / \partial x = 2x$. Partial with respect to $y$ (treating $x$ as a constant): $\partial f / \partial y = 2y$. Stacking them gives the gradient:

$$ \nabla f(x, y) \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 2x \\ 2y \end{bmatrix}. $$

Try a few points and see whether the answer matches your intuition for a bowl:

Point$\nabla f$ thereWhat it says
$(1, 0)$$(2, 0)$Steepest increase is straight along $+x$.
$(0, 1)$$(0, 2)$Steepest increase is straight along $+y$.
$(3, 4)$$(6, 8)$Steepest increase is outward, away from the origin.
$(0, 0)$$(0, 0)$You're at the bottom. There's no uphill — every direction is flat or up.

At every non-origin point, the gradient points away from the origin — that is, toward higher values of $f$. Exactly what we'd expect from a bowl whose lowest point is at the center.

Gradient descent: walking downhill

Now we have everything we need to state the central optimization rule of machine learning. Imagine being dropped onto a foggy hillside. You can't see far, but you can feel the slope under your feet. To get to the bottom, you don't need a map — you only need a strategy: take a small step in whatever direction feels most downhill, then look around and repeat. Eventually, you reach a valley.

That is gradient descent. The "feeling under your feet" is the gradient. The "small step" is something we control with a number called the learning rate.

Why a small step?

The gradient describes the slope right where you are standing, and nowhere else. Walk a long way and the hillside might curve, twist, or even start going up again. So we trust the gradient just enough to take a cautious step, then re-measure. Local information, used carefully and repeatedly.

The update rule

Let $\mathbf{w}$ be a vector containing all the parameters (the dials), and let $L(\mathbf{w})$ be the loss — the single number we're trying to drive down. The gradient descent update is:

$$ \mathbf{w}_{\text{new}} \;=\; \mathbf{w}_{\text{old}} \;-\; \eta\, \nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{\text{old}}). $$
SymbolPlain English
$\mathbf{w}$All the weights of the network, gathered into a single (possibly huge) vector.
$L(\mathbf{w})$The loss — one number measuring how wrong the network's predictions are.
$\nabla L(\mathbf{w})$The vector of partial derivatives of the loss with respect to every weight.
$\eta$ (eta)The learning rate — a small positive number controlling step size.
The minus signBecause $\nabla L$ points uphill, and we want to go down.

A worked numerical step

Take our bowl $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ as a stand-in for a loss function. Suppose we currently sit at the point $(x, y) = (3, 4)$. The value of $f$ there is $9 + 16 = 25$. Not great — we'd like to be closer to the bottom (the origin), where $f = 0$.

At $(3, 4)$ the gradient is $(2 \cdot 3,\; 2 \cdot 4) = (6, 8)$. With learning rate $\eta = 0.1$, the gradient descent update is:

$$ (x, y)_{\text{new}} \;=\; (3, 4) \;-\; 0.1 \cdot (6, 8) \;=\; (3 - 0.6,\; 4 - 0.8) \;=\; (2.4, 3.2). $$

And $f(2.4, 3.2) = 5.76 + 10.24 = 16.0$. Down from $25$. We're closer to the bottom. From $(2.4, 3.2)$ the gradient is $(4.8, 6.4)$, taking us next to $(1.92, 2.56)$ where $f = 10.24$. Lower again. Repeat enough times and we creep arbitrarily close to the origin. That's gradient descent in action — small step, re-measure, small step, re-measure.

A note on the learning rate

If $\eta$ is too small, training crawls. If $\eta$ is too large, the step overshoots and the loss can actually increase — like trying to climb down a hill in giant leaps and tumbling past the bottom. Picking $\eta$ well is one of the everyday arts of training neural networks. We'll come back to this in Chapter 7.

Why the gradient is perpendicular to contours

A contour (or level set) of $f$ is a curve along which $f$ stays constant — exactly the same idea as the elevation lines on a hiking map. If you walk along a contour, the height never changes; your footing stays at the same altitude the whole time.

Now consider the two extremes of "rate of change" at a single point:

  • Walking along the contour, $f$ doesn't change at all. Rate of change = $0$.
  • Walking in the direction of $\nabla f$, $f$ changes as fast as possible. Rate of change = maximum.

A direction with zero change and a direction with maximum change must be perpendicular to each other. (If they weren't, you could nudge your "zero change" direction a little toward the gradient and pick up some change — contradiction.) So:

The gradient always crosses contours at right angles.

That's why gradient descent, which follows $-\nabla f$, doesn't trace a contour — it cuts straight across them, dropping in $f$ as fast as the local geometry allows.

The multivariable chain rule

In Chapter 3 the chain rule said: if $y = f(g(x))$, then $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = f'(g(x))\, g'(x)$. In words, the derivative of a composition is the product of the inner and outer derivatives. We need a version that handles many inputs and many intermediate steps, because that's what a deep network is — a long chain of compositions.

Suppose $y = f(u, v)$ and both $u$ and $v$ depend on $x$. Then there are two paths from $x$ to $y$: one going through $u$, and one going through $v$. To get the total rate at which $y$ changes when $x$ moves, you add up the contributions of each path, where each path contributes the product of derivatives along it:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\, \frac{du}{dx}}_{\text{path through } u} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\, \frac{dv}{dx}}_{\text{path through } v}. $$

And if $u, v$ each depend on several variables $x_1, \ldots, x_m$, then you get one such equation per input, with partials throughout:

$$ \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_i} \;=\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\, \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_i} \;+\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\, \frac{\partial v}{\partial x_i}. $$
Read this slowly

The multivariable chain rule says: for each input variable, sum over all the paths through which it can influence the output. Each path contributes a product of partials, one factor per link in the chain. Backpropagation — the algorithm that trains modern neural networks — is just this rule, applied carefully through every layer of the network.

Jacobians: gradients for vector-valued functions

Sometimes a function takes a vector in and returns a single number — like the loss. Its gradient is a vector. But sometimes a function takes a vector in and returns another vector out — like a layer of a neural network, which transforms one list of activations into another. We need a way to bookkeep all the partials in this case too.

Suppose $\mathbf{f}$ takes $n$ inputs and produces $m$ outputs. There are $m$ output components, each depending on $n$ input components, so $m \times n$ partials in total. Stack them in a matrix:

$$ \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}} \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \partial f_1 / \partial x_1 & \cdots & \partial f_1 / \partial x_n \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \partial f_m / \partial x_1 & \cdots & \partial f_m / \partial x_n \end{bmatrix} \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}. $$

That's the Jacobian matrix. A useful way to read it:

  • Row $i$ describes how output component $f_i$ depends on each input — it's the gradient of $f_i$ written sideways.
  • Column $j$ describes how every output reacts when input $x_j$ wiggles.

With Jacobians, the messy summation form of the multivariable chain rule turns into clean matrix multiplication. If $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})$ and $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{y})$, then

$$ \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g} \circ \mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{y}) \cdot \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}). $$

Outer Jacobian on the left, inner Jacobian on the right. The shapes line up automatically — that's part of why this notation is so popular.

Worked example: a linear function

Let $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$, where $\mathbf{A}$ is a fixed matrix of numbers. The $i$-th component is just a weighted sum:

$$ y_i \;=\; \sum_j A_{ij}\, x_j, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial x_j} \;=\; A_{ij}. $$

The Jacobian is therefore the matrix $\mathbf{A}$ itself:

$$ \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \mathbf{A}. $$

This is the matrix version of the familiar fact that the derivative of $mx + b$ is $m$. Linear functions are their own derivatives, even when they live in many dimensions.

What the gradient of the loss looks like

Now zoom out and put it all together. The loss $L$ takes the parameters $\mathbf{w}$ (every weight in the network, gathered into one giant vector) and returns a single number measuring error. So $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} L$ is a vector with the same shape as $\mathbf{w}$ — one partial derivative per parameter. If the network has 175 billion parameters, the gradient has 175 billion components. A scary-sounding object, but each component is just one ordinary partial derivative.

Two facts make this object useful in practice:

  • It lives in the same space as the parameters. That means we can subtract a small multiple of it directly from $\mathbf{w}$. The result is just another valid set of weights — slightly better than the ones we started with.
  • It tells the network how to improve. Each component answers, for one specific weight, the question "should I go up or down, and by how much, to make the loss smaller?"
Why optimization matters

A neural network at initialization is just a pile of random numbers — its predictions are nonsense. What turns it into something useful is the process of repeatedly computing the gradient of the loss and stepping the weights in the downhill direction. There is no magic in the architecture alone; the magic is in the optimization. The model learns because the gradient keeps pointing the way down.

A peek at backpropagation

We have not yet said how to compute the gradient when the network has a billion weights. Doing it naively — by perturbing each weight one at a time and re-running the whole network — would be hopelessly slow. Fortunately, there's a much smarter way.

A deep neural network is a long composition of simple functions: input → layer 1 → layer 2 → … → output → loss. The multivariable chain rule from earlier in this chapter tells us how to differentiate compositions. If we apply it carefully, layer by layer, working backward from the loss to the inputs, we can compute every partial derivative of $L$ in roughly the same time it took to compute $L$ itself.

That algorithm has a name: backpropagation. It's the chain rule, organized cleverly, executed in reverse. We'll spend an entire chapter on it later. For now, just hold on to the picture: the gradient isn't computed by brute force — it flows backward through the network, one layer at a time, each step using nothing more than the chain rule we already know.

Check yourself

  1. If $f(x, y) = x^2 y$, what are $\partial f / \partial x$ and $\partial f / \partial y$?
  2. What is $\nabla f$ for the function $f(x, y) = \tfrac{1}{2}(x^2 + y^2)$? At the point $(1, 1)$, which direction does the gradient point?
  3. Why is $-\nabla L$ the right direction to step when we want the loss to decrease?
  4. If $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$, what is the Jacobian $\partial \mathbf{y} / \partial \mathbf{x}$?
  5. Two functions are composed; their Jacobians multiply. In what order?
  6. You're at point $(2, 1)$ on the bowl $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$, and you take one gradient descent step with learning rate $\eta = 0.25$. Where do you land, and how much did $f$ drop?

Answers:

  1. $\partial f / \partial x = 2xy$ and $\partial f / \partial y = x^2$.
  2. $\nabla f = (x, y)$. At $(1, 1)$ it points in the direction $(1, 1)$ — diagonally outward from the origin, which is uphill on the bowl.
  3. Because $\nabla L$ points in the steepest uphill direction; its negative therefore points in the steepest downhill direction — the fastest way to reduce the loss locally.
  4. The Jacobian is $\mathbf{A}$. The constant $\mathbf{b}$ contributes zero to every partial.
  5. Outer first, then inner: $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})) \cdot \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x})$.
  6. The gradient at $(2, 1)$ is $(4, 2)$. The update is $(2, 1) - 0.25 \cdot (4, 2) = (1, 0.5)$. The loss drops from $f(2,1) = 5$ to $f(1, 0.5) = 1.25$.
