Multivariable & Gradients

Functions of many variables. Partial derivatives. The gradient — a vector that points in the direction of steepest increase. The chain rule when everything is a vector.

Why we need this

The loss of a neural network depends on millions of parameters at once. We need calculus that works on a function $L(w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n)$ — a single number that depends on a long list of inputs. The single-variable derivative from Chapter 3 still applies; we just have to apply it carefully, one input at a time.

Partial derivatives

Let $f(x, y) = x^2 + 3xy + y^3$. To compute the partial derivative of $f$ with respect to $x$, we treat every other variable as a constant and differentiate as usual:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x + 3y. $$

Likewise, for the partial with respect to $y$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 3x + 3y^2. $$

The curly $\partial$ is read "partial." It's just a reminder that the function has other variables you are deliberately holding fixed.

Intuition

The partial $\partial f / \partial x$ at a point $(x_0, y_0)$ tells you: if I freeze $y$ at $y_0$ and slide $x$ a tiny bit, how fast does $f$ change? Slice the surface $f$ with a vertical plane parallel to the $x$-axis; the partial is the slope of the resulting curve.

The gradient

Bundle all the partials into a single vector and you get the gradient:

$$ \nabla f \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \partial f / \partial x_1 \\ \partial f / \partial x_2 \\ \vdots \\ \partial f / \partial x_n \end{bmatrix}. $$

This is a vector-valued thing: at every point in the input space it is a vector with the same dimension as the input. Two facts make it indispensable.

  1. The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase of $f$. There is no other direction in which $f$ grows faster.
  2. Its length tells you how fast $f$ grows along that steepest direction.

So the negative gradient, $-\nabla f$, points in the direction of steepest decrease. That is exactly what we want when we are trying to drive a loss down. Gradient descent in Chapter 7 is one short formula:

$$ \mathbf{w}_{\text{new}} \;=\; \mathbf{w}_{\text{old}} \;-\; \eta\, \nabla L(\mathbf{w}_{\text{old}}), $$

where $\eta$ is a small positive learning rate. Take a small step downhill; repeat.

Why the gradient is perpendicular to contours

A contour (level set) of $f$ is a curve where $f$ stays constant. If you walk along a contour, $f$ doesn't change — its derivative along the walk is zero. The gradient $\nabla f$ tells you the direction along which $f$ changes fastest. A direction with zero change must be perpendicular to a direction with maximum change. Hence: gradients sit at right angles to contours.

This is also why "steepest descent" doesn't trace a contour — it crosses contours at right angles, dropping in $f$ as fast as possible.

The multivariable chain rule

Single-variable: if $y = f(g(x))$, then $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = f'(g(x))\, g'(x)$.

What if $y = f(u, v)$ and both $u$ and $v$ depend on $x$? You add up the contributions through each path:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\, \frac{du}{dx} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\, \frac{dv}{dx}. $$

And if $u, v$ each depend on several variables $x_1, \ldots, x_m$? You get one such equation per $x_i$, with partials throughout:

$$ \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_i} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\, \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_i} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\, \frac{\partial v}{\partial x_i}. $$
Read this twice

The multivariable chain rule says: for each input variable, sum over all paths through which it influences the output. Each path contributes a product of partials, one per link. Backpropagation is exactly this rule, scaled up to a network of paths.

Jacobians

Now consider a function $\mathbf{f} : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ — input is a vector, output is a vector. We have $m$ output components, each depending on $n$ input components. Stack all the partials in a matrix:

$$ \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}} = \begin{bmatrix} \partial f_1 / \partial x_1 & \cdots & \partial f_1 / \partial x_n \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \partial f_m / \partial x_1 & \cdots & \partial f_m / \partial x_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}. $$

That's the Jacobian matrix. Row $i$ is the gradient of the $i$-th output (transposed); column $j$ tells you how all outputs depend on $x_j$. The vector chain rule reads neatly as a matrix product: if $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})$ and $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{y})$, then

$$ \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g} \circ \mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{y}) \cdot \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}). $$

Notice the order: outer Jacobian on the left, inner Jacobian on the right. The matrix multiplication enforces the shape constraints automatically.

Worked example

Let $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ where $\mathbf{A}$ is a fixed matrix. The $i$-th component is $y_i = \sum_j A_{ij} x_j$, so $\partial y_i / \partial x_j = A_{ij}$. The Jacobian is just $\mathbf{A}$ itself:

$$ \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{A}. $$

Linear functions are their own derivatives. (This is the matrix version of "the derivative of $mx + b$ is $m$.")

What the gradient of the loss looks like

The loss $L$ takes the parameters $\mathbf{w}$ and returns a single number. Its gradient $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} L$ is therefore a vector of the same shape as $\mathbf{w}$, one partial derivative per parameter. If your model has 175 billion parameters, the gradient is a 175-billion-dimensional vector — a structured object, but each component is just one partial.

Two practical points carry forward:

  • The gradient lives in the same space as the parameters. You can add a small multiple of $-\nabla L$ to the parameters; that's the update step.
  • To compute the gradient efficiently, we apply the chain rule layer by layer, in reverse. That algorithm is backpropagation.

Check yourself

  1. If $f(x,y) = x^2 y$, what are $\partial f/\partial x$ and $\partial f/\partial y$?
  2. What is $\nabla f$ for the function $f(x,y) = \tfrac12(x^2 + y^2)$?
  3. Why is $-\nabla L$ the right direction for "downhill"?
  4. If $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$, what is $\partial \mathbf{y} / \partial \mathbf{x}$?
  5. Two functions composed; their Jacobians multiply. In what order?

Answers: $2xy$ and $x^2$; $\nabla f = (x, y)$ — a vector pointing radially outward from the origin; because $\nabla L$ is uphill, its negative is downhill, the steepest downhill at that; the Jacobian is $\mathbf{A}$; outer first, then inner: $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})) \, \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x})$.
