Linear Algebra

Vectors as arrows. Matrices as machines that transform space. The dot product, which is half the work of a neuron.

Why vectors

A neural network rarely takes one number as input. It takes a list: the 784 pixel values of a small image, the 300 numbers describing a word's "meaning," the 4096 features of a hidden layer. Doing arithmetic on long lists is tedious if we write it out one component at a time. Linear algebra is the language for doing it compactly.

Two questions to keep in mind throughout this chapter:

  • What does a vector represent? Sometimes a point in space, sometimes a direction, sometimes a list of features.
  • What does a matrix do? A matrix is a function that takes a vector and produces another vector — a transformation of space.

Vectors

A vector is an ordered list of numbers. We write it as a column:

$$ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. $$

The boldface $\mathbf{v}$ tells you it is a vector, not a single number. The number of entries is the dimension; we say $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ (a vector in 3-dimensional real space). In a neural network the dimension can be in the thousands.

Geometrically, a 2D or 3D vector is most easily pictured as an arrow from the origin to the point with those coordinates. It has a length and a direction. In higher dimensions we lose the picture, but the arithmetic is identical.

Vector addition and scaling

Two operations on vectors do everything else:

  1. Addition: add component-by-component. $$ \begin{bmatrix} 1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 4\\-1\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5\\1\\3\end{bmatrix}. $$ Geometrically, place the second arrow at the tip of the first; their sum is the arrow from the start of the first to the tip of the second.
  2. Scalar multiplication: multiply every component by a single number $c$ (a "scalar"). $$ 2 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1\\2\\3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\\4\\6 \end{bmatrix}. $$ Geometrically, this stretches the arrow (or flips and stretches it if $c < 0$).

Any vector you can reach from a starting set using only these two operations is called a linear combination of the starting set. This is the entire content of the word "linear" in linear algebra.

The dot product

Take two vectors of the same dimension. Multiply them element-by-element, then sum:

$$ \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i. $$

Concretely:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; (2)(1) + (-1)(4) + (3)(2) \;=\; 4. $$

That tiny formula is the heart of every neural network. A single neuron computes a dot product between an input vector $\mathbf{x}$ and a weight vector $\mathbf{w}$, adds a bias, and passes the result through a nonlinearity. We will see this verbatim in Chapter 6.

Two readings of the dot product

Algebraic: sum the products of matching entries.

Geometric: $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\, \|\mathbf{b}\| \cos\theta$ where $\theta$ is the angle between them. So a dot product measures "how much the two vectors point the same way." Aligned: large positive. Perpendicular: zero. Opposite: large negative.

Length and unit vectors

The length (or norm) of a vector is its Pythagorean distance from the origin:

$$ \|\mathbf{v}\| \;=\; \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \cdots + v_n^2} \;=\; \sqrt{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}. $$

A unit vector is one with length $1$. To make a unit vector pointing in the direction of $\mathbf{v}$, divide by its length: $\mathbf{v} / \|\mathbf{v}\|$. We will use this when we talk about gradients (Chapter 4) and attention (Chapter 13).

Matrices

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers. We write a matrix with capital boldface and specify its shape as (rows) by (columns):

$$ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3}. $$

A vector is just a special matrix with a single column. So $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the same as $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 1}$ if you want.

The most useful way to think about a matrix is as a function on vectors: feed it a vector, get a vector back. The function is called matrix-vector multiplication.

Matrix-vector multiplication

If $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ and $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the product $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^m$. Its $i$-th entry is the dot product of the $i$-th row of $\mathbf{A}$ with $\mathbf{x}$:

$$ (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})_i \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{n} A_{ij}\, x_j. $$

Worked example. Let

$$ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \\ -1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Then

$$ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2(5)+1(2) \\ 0(5)+3(2) \\ -1(5)+4(2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 \\ 6 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Two important shape rules:

  • The number of columns of $\mathbf{A}$ must equal the dimension of $\mathbf{x}$.
  • The result has as many entries as $\mathbf{A}$ has rows.

In neural-network notation, $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$ is one whole layer's worth of computation. Each row of $\mathbf{W}$ is a single neuron's weights; each entry of $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}$ is one neuron's pre-activation.

A matrix as a transformation

A matrix is more than a grid of numbers — it is a rule for moving every point in 2D space to a new point. The grid encodes the rule, but the rule itself is geometric. Once you can see it, the algebra in the previous section stops feeling arbitrary. We'll build the picture in three steps: basis vectors, the unit square, and the rule that ties them together.

Basis vectors

The two simplest column vectors in 2D are

$$ \mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

$\mathbf{e}_1$ is one step along the $x$-axis and $\mathbf{e}_2$ is one step along the $y$-axis. They are called the standard basis because every column vector

$$ \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} $$

is built out of them by stacking the entries one above the other:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; x_1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + x_2 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; x_1\,\mathbf{e}_1 + x_2\,\mathbf{e}_2. $$

Concrete example: $\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; 3\,\mathbf{e}_1 + 2\,\mathbf{e}_2$ — three units along $x$, two units along $y$. This will matter in a moment: if a transformation tells us where $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$ land, that's enough to know where every column vector lands, because every column vector is just a recipe of how many $\mathbf{e}_1$'s and how many $\mathbf{e}_2$'s to combine.

The unit square

The simplest closed shape in 2D is the unit square — the square whose four corners we collect into a single $2 \times 4$ matrix, one corner per column:

$$ \mathbf{S} \;=\; \Bigl[\; \mathbf{c}_1 \;\; \mathbf{c}_2 \;\; \mathbf{c}_3 \;\; \mathbf{c}_4 \;\Bigr] \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Reading the columns: $\mathbf{c}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ is the origin, $\mathbf{c}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{c}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{c}_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Its two edges starting at the origin are exactly $\mathbf{e}_1$ (the bottom edge) and $\mathbf{e}_2$ (the left edge). We use it as a test shape: if we apply a matrix to its four corners and watch where they land, the new shape gives us a complete picture of what the matrix does to all of 2D.

The rule

Read the columns

The columns of $\mathbf{A}$ are where the basis vectors land. The first column says where $\mathbf{e}_1$ goes. The second column says where $\mathbf{e}_2$ goes. That's it — once you know those two columns, you know what $\mathbf{A}$ does to every column vector.

Why is this true? Compute $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{e}_1$ directly:

$$ \mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{e}_1 \;=\; \mathbf{A}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; 1 \cdot (\text{first column of } \mathbf{A}) + 0 \cdot (\text{second column of } \mathbf{A}) \;=\; \text{first column of } \mathbf{A}. $$

Same idea for $\mathbf{e}_2$ — the multiplication picks out the second column. And because matrix multiplication is linear,

$$ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} \;=\; \mathbf{A}\bigl(x_1\,\mathbf{e}_1 + x_2\,\mathbf{e}_2\bigr) \;=\; x_1\,(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{e}_1) + x_2\,(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{e}_2). $$

So $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ is the same combination of the two new columns that $\mathbf{x}$ was of $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$. The whole game is reading the columns.

Worked example

Take

$$ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0.5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Reading the columns as instructions:

  • $\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ becomes $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ — twice as long along the $x$-axis, still on the $x$-axis.
  • $\mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ becomes $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ — same height, but tilted toward $+x$.

In plain English: $\mathbf{A}$ stretches the plane horizontally by a factor of $2$ and shears it so that what used to be straight up now leans rightward.

Now watch each corner of the unit square. Every corner is a tiny recipe of $\mathbf{e}_1$'s and $\mathbf{e}_2$'s, so its new location is the same recipe of the two new columns:

Original corner As a combination New corner
$\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ $0\,\mathbf{e}_1 + 0\,\mathbf{e}_2$ $\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$  — the origin never moves
$\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ $\mathbf{e}_1$ $\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$
$\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ $\mathbf{e}_2$ $\begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
$\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ $\mathbf{e}_1 + \mathbf{e}_2$ $\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Connect the four new corners and you get a parallelogram whose two sides at the origin are the columns of $\mathbf{A}$. The shape is no longer a square because the columns are no longer perpendicular.

A common confusion

The two columns of $\mathbf{A}$ are not two transformations applied one after the other ("first stretch by $(2, 0)$, then shear by $(0.5, 1)$"). They are two destinations: where $\mathbf{e}_1$ ends up, and where $\mathbf{e}_2$ ends up. The matrix is a single transformation, fully described once you know those two columns.

Two takeaways:

  • Matrix multiplication is linear: $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{A}(c\,\mathbf{x}) = c \,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$. (We just used this to track the corners.)
  • To read a matrix at a glance, look at its columns. Each column is the destination of one basis vector.

This is exactly how to read a neural network's weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$. Each column of $\mathbf{W}$ tells you where one input direction lands in the layer's output space — the same picture, just in higher dimensions. A whole layer is a transformation that bends input space, and the network is many such transformations stacked back-to-back with nonlinearities in between.

Matrix-matrix multiplication

Composing two transformations is itself a transformation, and we represent it by multiplying the two matrices. If $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ and $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$, then $\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p}$ with

$$ C_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} A_{ik}\, B_{kj}. $$

Same shape rule as before: inner dimensions must match ($n$ here). Read it as "row $i$ of $\mathbf{A}$ dotted with column $j$ of $\mathbf{B}$."

A two-layer neural network is just $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}$ with a nonlinearity inserted somewhere — without the nonlinearity, it would collapse into a single matrix because $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ is itself a matrix. We will see that point made carefully in Chapter 8.

The transpose

Flipping a matrix on its diagonal — turning row $i$ into column $i$ — is called the transpose, written $\mathbf{A}^\top$. An $m \times n$ matrix becomes $n \times m$. A small example:

$$ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \mathbf{A}^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Two algebraic identities you will use constantly:

$$ (\mathbf{A}^\top)^\top = \mathbf{A}, \qquad (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^\top = \mathbf{B}^\top \mathbf{A}^\top. $$

The order flip in the second identity is the rule that propagates transposes through long matrix products in backpropagation derivations. So why do we transpose in the first place?

Why we transpose

1. Dot product as a matrix product. A row vector ($1 \times n$) times a column vector ($n \times 1$) gives a single number — exactly the dot product. So if $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are both column vectors,

$$ \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} \;=\; \mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{b}. $$

This single notational trick lets us write entire layers without a separate "dot" symbol — everything becomes matrix multiplication.

2. Reversing the direction of a linear map. A matrix $\mathbf{W}$ of shape $m \times n$ takes inputs (in $\mathbb{R}^n$) to outputs (in $\mathbb{R}^m$). Often we need the opposite-direction map — taking something living in output space and pushing it back to input space. The shape that fits is $n \times m$, which is exactly $\mathbf{W}^\top$. This is precisely the situation in backpropagation: an error signal lives in the output space, and to update the inputs or the weights we have to push that signal back through the layer. The matrix that does so is automatically $\mathbf{W}^\top$.

3. Symmetry. A matrix that satisfies $\mathbf{A}^\top = \mathbf{A}$ is called symmetric. Two important examples appear later: covariance matrices (Chapter 5) and Hessians of scalar functions. Symmetric matrices have especially clean structure — real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors — which is why optimization theory leans on them.

4. Shape bookkeeping. Often you have an expression like $\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{A} \mathbf{y}$ and need to know its shape. Reading left to right: $1 \times n$ times $n \times m$ times $m \times 1$ collapses to $1 \times 1$ — a single number (a quadratic form). The transposes are how you make the dimensions line up so the product even exists.

A glimpse of backpropagation

When you take a gradient of $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}$ with respect to $\mathbf{x}$, the matrix $\mathbf{W}$ shows up transposed. This is not magic; it is bookkeeping forced by the chain rule — the rule for differentiating a composition of functions. We introduce it in Chapter 3 (single-variable) and extend it to vectors and matrices in Chapter 4. Every backpropagation derivation in Chapter 9 then uses this fact.

Vectors as data, matrices as parameters

A useful mental model going forward:

  • A vector is usually one example: one image's pixels, one word's embedding, one neuron layer's activations.
  • A matrix is usually a parameter: the weights of a layer connecting one set of activations to the next. Or, when you stack many examples into one big object, it is a batch of vectors.

The shape conventions for "vector of inputs $\to$ matrix of weights $\to$ vector of outputs" feel arbitrary at first; once you have done a few worked examples (Chapters 6 and 8) they become second nature.

Check yourself

  1. What is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$?
  2. What is the length of $(3, 4)$?
  3. If $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 7}$ and $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^7$, what shape is $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$?
  4. What does "the dot product is zero" mean geometrically?
  5. Why must matrix multiplication match inner dimensions?

Answers: $4 + 0 - 3 = 1$; $\sqrt{9+16}=5$; a vector in $\mathbb{R}^4$; the two vectors are perpendicular; because the inner dimension counts how many products are summed, and that has to be a single agreed-upon number.
