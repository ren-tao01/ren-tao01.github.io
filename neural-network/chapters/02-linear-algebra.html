Linear Algebra

Vectors as arrows. Matrices as machines that transform space. The dot product, which is half the work of a neuron.

Why vectors

A neural network rarely takes one number as input. It takes a list: the 784 pixel values of a small image, the 300 numbers describing a word's "meaning," the 4096 features of a hidden layer. Doing arithmetic on long lists is tedious if we write it out one component at a time. Linear algebra is the language for doing it compactly.

Two questions to keep in mind throughout this chapter:

  • What does a vector represent? Sometimes a point in space, sometimes a direction, sometimes a list of features.
  • What does a matrix do? A matrix is a function that takes a vector and produces another vector — a transformation of space.

Vectors

A vector is an ordered list of numbers. We write it as a column:

$$ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. $$

The boldface $\mathbf{v}$ tells you it is a vector, not a single number. The number of entries is the dimension; we say $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ (a vector in 3-dimensional real space). In a neural network the dimension can be in the thousands.

Geometrically, a 2D or 3D vector is most easily pictured as an arrow from the origin to the point with those coordinates. It has a length and a direction. In higher dimensions we lose the picture, but the arithmetic is identical.

Vector addition and scaling

Two operations on vectors do everything else:

  1. Addition: add component-by-component. $$ \begin{bmatrix} 1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 4\\-1\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5\\1\\3\end{bmatrix}. $$ Geometrically, place the second arrow at the tip of the first; their sum is the arrow from the start of the first to the tip of the second.
  2. Scalar multiplication: multiply every component by a single number $c$ (a "scalar"). $$ 2 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1\\2\\3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\\4\\6 \end{bmatrix}. $$ Geometrically, this stretches the arrow (or flips and stretches it if $c < 0$).

Any vector you can reach from a starting set using only these two operations is called a linear combination of the starting set. This is the entire content of the word "linear" in linear algebra.

The dot product

Take two vectors of the same dimension. Multiply them element-by-element, then sum:

$$ \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i. $$

Concretely:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; (2)(1) + (-1)(4) + (3)(2) \;=\; 4. $$

That tiny formula is the heart of every neural network. A single neuron computes a dot product between an input vector $\mathbf{x}$ and a weight vector $\mathbf{w}$, adds a bias, and passes the result through a nonlinearity. We will see this verbatim in Chapter 6.

Two readings of the dot product

Algebraic: sum the products of matching entries.

Geometric: $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\, \|\mathbf{b}\| \cos\theta$ where $\theta$ is the angle between them. So a dot product measures "how much the two vectors point the same way." Aligned: large positive. Perpendicular: zero. Opposite: large negative.

Length and unit vectors

The length (or norm) of a vector is its Pythagorean distance from the origin:

$$ \|\mathbf{v}\| \;=\; \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \cdots + v_n^2} \;=\; \sqrt{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}. $$

A unit vector is one with length $1$. To make a unit vector pointing in the direction of $\mathbf{v}$, divide by its length: $\mathbf{v} / \|\mathbf{v}\|$. We will use this when we talk about gradients (Chapter 4) and attention (Chapter 13).

Matrices

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers. We write a matrix with capital boldface and specify its shape as (rows) by (columns):

$$ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3}. $$

A vector is just a special matrix with a single column. So $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the same as $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 1}$ if you want.

The most useful way to think about a matrix is as a function on vectors: feed it a vector, get a vector back. The function is called matrix-vector multiplication.

Matrix-vector multiplication

If $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ and $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the product $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^m$. Its $i$-th entry is the dot product of the $i$-th row of $\mathbf{A}$ with $\mathbf{x}$:

$$ (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})_i \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{n} A_{ij}\, x_j. $$

Worked example. Let

$$ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \\ -1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Then

$$ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2(5)+1(2) \\ 0(5)+3(2) \\ -1(5)+4(2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 \\ 6 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Two important shape rules:

  • The number of columns of $\mathbf{A}$ must equal the dimension of $\mathbf{x}$.
  • The result has as many entries as $\mathbf{A}$ has rows.

In neural-network notation, $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$ is one whole layer's worth of computation. Each row of $\mathbf{W}$ is a single neuron's weights; each entry of $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}$ is one neuron's pre-activation.

A matrix as a transformation

Try this. Take the four corners of the unit square in 2D — the points $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(1,1)$, $(0,1)$ — and apply

$$ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0.5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Each corner becomes a new point. Draw the new shape and you'll see a parallelogram: the square has been stretched horizontally by a factor of $2$ and skewed.

Two facts that follow:

  • Matrix multiplication is linear: $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{A}(c\,\mathbf{x}) = c \,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$.
  • The columns of $\mathbf{A}$ tell you where the basis vectors $\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2, \ldots$ end up. Once you know that, the action on every other vector is determined by linearity.

Matrix-matrix multiplication

Composing two transformations is itself a transformation, and we represent it by multiplying the two matrices. If $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ and $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$, then $\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p}$ with

$$ C_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} A_{ik}\, B_{kj}. $$

Same shape rule as before: inner dimensions must match ($n$ here). Read it as "row $i$ of $\mathbf{A}$ dotted with column $j$ of $\mathbf{B}$."

A two-layer neural network is just $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}$ with a nonlinearity inserted somewhere — without the nonlinearity, it would collapse into a single matrix because $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ is itself a matrix. We will see that point made carefully in Chapter 8.

The transpose

Flipping a matrix on its diagonal — turning rows into columns — is called the transpose, written $\mathbf{A}^\top$. It satisfies $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^\top = \mathbf{B}^\top \mathbf{A}^\top$ (note the order flip) and $(\mathbf{A}^\top)^\top = \mathbf{A}$. The dot product can be written as a matrix product: $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{b}$.

We will see the transpose enter every backpropagation derivation in Chapter 9. When you take a gradient of a matrix-vector multiplication, the matrix shows up transposed. This is not magic; it is bookkeeping forced by the chain rule.

Vectors as data, matrices as parameters

A useful mental model going forward:

  • A vector is usually one example: one image's pixels, one word's embedding, one neuron layer's activations.
  • A matrix is usually a parameter: the weights of a layer connecting one set of activations to the next. Or, when you stack many examples into one big object, it is a batch of vectors.

The shape conventions for "vector of inputs $\to$ matrix of weights $\to$ vector of outputs" feel arbitrary at first; once you have done a few worked examples (Chapters 6 and 8) they become second nature.

Check yourself

  1. What is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$?
  2. What is the length of $(3, 4)$?
  3. If $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 7}$ and $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^7$, what shape is $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$?
  4. What does "the dot product is zero" mean geometrically?
  5. Why must matrix multiplication match inner dimensions?

Answers: $4 + 0 - 3 = 1$; $\sqrt{9+16}=5$; a vector in $\mathbb{R}^4$; the two vectors are perpendicular; because the inner dimension counts how many products are summed, and that has to be a single agreed-upon number.
