Probability & Information

Random variables. Mean and variance. Entropy. Cross-entropy. KL divergence. The handful of quantities that decide what "loss" actually measures when our outputs are probabilities.

Why probability shows up in deep learning

Most useful neural networks output probabilities. A classifier reports "85% confident this is a cat." A language model reports "the next token is the with probability 0.13." Once outputs are probabilities, the loss function should also speak the language of probability — distances between distributions, surprises, expectations. That is what this chapter sets up.

Random variables

A random variable is just a label for a number whose value is uncertain — perhaps it depends on a coin flip, perhaps on which sample we drew from a dataset. We say a random variable $X$ takes value $x$ with probability $P(X = x)$.

Two flavors:

  • Discrete: $X$ takes finitely (or countably) many values. We give a probability to each. The list of probabilities is called the probability mass function, written $p(x)$. They must be nonnegative and sum to 1.
  • Continuous: $X$ takes values in an interval. Probability is described by a probability density function $p(x) \ge 0$ that integrates to 1. The probability of landing exactly on any single point is zero; only intervals carry probability.

Expectation and variance

The expectation (or mean) of $X$ is the probability-weighted average of its possible values:

$$ \mathbb{E}[X] \;=\; \sum_{x} x\, p(x) \quad\text{(discrete)}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\, p(x)\, dx \quad\text{(continuous)}. $$

The variance measures spread — how far $X$ tends to land from its mean:

$$ \mathrm{Var}[X] \;=\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\bigr]. $$

Its square root is the standard deviation. We will care about variance in Chapter 10 — variance of activations and gradients drives whether networks train at all.

Independence

Two random variables $X$ and $Y$ are independent if knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Equivalently, the joint probability factorizes:

$$ P(X = x, Y = y) \;=\; P(X = x)\, P(Y = y). $$

The "joint probability is a product" is exactly why logarithms are natural for working with probabilities: $\ln$ of a product becomes a sum.

Conditional probability and Bayes

The conditional probability of $X$ given $Y$ is

$$ P(X = x \mid Y = y) \;=\; \frac{P(X = x, Y = y)}{P(Y = y)}. $$

Bayes' rule rearranges this into a useful form when you can compute one direction but want the other:

$$ P(X \mid Y) \;=\; \frac{P(Y \mid X)\, P(X)}{P(Y)}. $$

We will not use Bayes' theorem directly in this site. We mention it because maximum likelihood training — the principle behind the cross-entropy loss in Chapter 7 — is a Bayesian-flavored idea trimmed of priors.

Surprise: $-\ln p$

Suppose you observe an outcome $x$ that had probability $p(x)$. How surprised should you be? Two desiderata:

  1. An outcome with probability $1$ is no surprise: surprise should be $0$.
  2. Two independent surprises should add: if I see two independent rare events, I should be exactly as surprised as their individual surprises summed.

The unique function (up to a positive constant) that satisfies both is $-\ln p$. We call it surprise or information content. It's high for unlikely events and zero when $p = 1$.

Entropy

The entropy $H(p)$ of a distribution $p$ is the expected surprise — how surprised you are on average about an outcome drawn from $p$:

$$ H(p) \;=\; -\sum_{x} p(x)\, \ln p(x). $$

Three useful facts:

  • $H(p) \ge 0$, with equality only when $p$ is concentrated on a single outcome (no surprise).
  • For a fixed support of $n$ outcomes, $H(p)$ is largest when $p$ is uniform — that's the maximum-uncertainty distribution.
  • Entropy is the average number of nats needed to encode an outcome from $p$ (or bits, if you swap $\ln$ for $\log_2$).

Cross-entropy

Suppose the truth is $p$ (e.g. the actual one-hot label "this image is a cat"), but our model believes $q$ (e.g. it predicts $0.7$ cat, $0.2$ dog, $0.1$ horse). The cross-entropy measures the average surprise our model would feel about samples actually drawn from $p$:

$$ H(p, q) \;=\; -\sum_{x} p(x) \ln q(x). $$

The smaller this is, the better our model's beliefs match reality. Notice the asymmetry: we average using the true distribution but compute surprise using the predicted probabilities. That asymmetry is exactly why cross-entropy is a directional loss.

For a single training example with true label $y$ — a one-hot vector — and predicted probabilities $\hat{y}_1, \ldots, \hat{y}_C$, this collapses to the familiar negative-log-likelihood:

$$ \text{loss} \;=\; -\ln \hat{y}_{\text{correct class}}. $$

Picture: the network is rewarded by how much probability it places on the right class. Probability $1$ gives loss $0$. Probability $\to 0$ gives loss $\to \infty$. That severe punishment for confident wrong answers is what trains networks to be calibrated.

KL divergence

The Kullback–Leibler divergence from $q$ to $p$ measures how far $q$ is from $p$, in a probability-aware way:

$$ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) \;=\; \sum_x p(x)\, \ln \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \;=\; H(p, q) - H(p). $$

Two things to remember:

  • $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $p = q$.
  • It is not symmetric: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) \neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p)$ in general. It is not a distance in the metric sense, but it acts like one for our purposes.

Since $H(p)$ doesn't depend on the model, minimizing cross-entropy is the same as minimizing KL divergence: both push the model's distribution $q$ toward the truth $p$.

The softmax outputs probabilities; cross-entropy scores them

We will meet the softmax in Chapter 6: it turns a vector of real numbers into a vector of probabilities that sum to 1. When you stack a softmax output and a cross-entropy loss you get the standard classification head of a neural network:

$$ \hat{y}_k \;=\; \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j} e^{z_j}}, \qquad \text{loss} \;=\; -\sum_{k} y_k \ln \hat{y}_k. $$

It works so well partly because the gradient simplifies enormously — the messy logs cancel pieces of the messy exponentials. We will work that out in Chapter 9.

Check yourself

  1. What is $\mathbb{E}[X]$ for a fair six-sided die?
  2. If $p$ is concentrated entirely on one outcome, what is $H(p)$?
  3. Why is $-\ln p(x)$ a sensible measure of surprise?
  4. Why does minimizing cross-entropy minimize KL divergence?
  5. Why are logarithms helpful when working with probabilities of independent events?

Answers: $3.5$; $0$ (no surprise); because it equals $0$ for certain events, grows for unlikely ones, and turns products of independent probabilities into sums; because $H(p,q) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ and $H(p)$ is constant; because $\ln$ turns products into sums and avoids underflow.
