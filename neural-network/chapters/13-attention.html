Attention

A differentiable lookup table. Queries, keys, and values. The single equation that makes Transformers work.

The motivation, restated

An RNN forces every output to depend on a single fixed-size summary of all earlier inputs. That summary is the bottleneck. Attention removes the bottleneck by letting each output position look directly at every input position and decide for itself which parts to focus on.

The trick: the "decide which parts to focus on" step is differentiable, so the focusing rule is itself learnable. Backprop tunes it just like any other parameter.

Queries, keys, and values

Forget neural networks for a moment. Imagine a Python dictionary: 

scores = {"hello": 1, "world": 2, "code": 3}
scores["world"]   # → 2

Three pieces: a query (the string you look up), keys (the dictionary's keys), and values (what comes back). Hard lookup matches the query against exactly one key.

Soft lookup. What if instead of an exact match, every key returned a similarity score, and we took a weighted combination of all the values? That's attention. Replace strings with vectors; replace exact match with a dot product; replace "pick one" with "softmax over similarities." Done.

One-line summary

Attention is a differentiable lookup table where the address is computed by similarity, not equality.

Scaled dot-product attention

We have $n$ "queries," each a vector of dimension $d_k$, stacked as rows of a matrix $\mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_k}$. We have $m$ "keys" $\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d_k}$ and $m$ matching "values" $\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d_v}$. Attention is one formula:

$$ \mathrm{Attn}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) \;=\; \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\, \mathbf{V}. $$

Read it left to right:

  1. $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ — for every (query, key) pair, compute their dot product. Result: an $n \times m$ matrix of similarity scores.
  2. Divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$ — keep the scores from blowing up when $d_k$ is large (this is the "scaled" in the name).
  3. Apply softmax along each row — turn each row of scores into a probability distribution over the $m$ keys.
  4. Multiply by $\mathbf{V}$ — each output is the weighted average of the values, with the row's softmax weights.

The output has shape $n \times d_v$: one vector per query, each one a custom mixture of the values.

Self-attention

In self-attention, the queries, keys, and values all come from the same input sequence — each token attends to every other token (and to itself). Concretely, given a sequence $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, we compute

$$ \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_Q, \quad \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_K, \quad \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_V, $$

then run scaled dot-product attention on those. The matrices $\mathbf{W}_Q, \mathbf{W}_K, \mathbf{W}_V$ are learnable parameters. Each token's output is a context-aware summary of the whole sequence: tokens with high similarity to the current token get pulled in heavily; irrelevant tokens get downweighted.

Notice what self-attention is doing for free:

  • It is permutation-equivariant (without positional encoding, more on that in Chapter 14): treating tokens as a set, not a sequence.
  • It can mix information across arbitrary distances in one layer — no signal-decay-with-depth as in RNNs.
  • It is fully parallel: every output is computed independently, given $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}$.

Why divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$

If the entries of $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ are independent random numbers with mean $0$ and variance $1$, the dot product $\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{k} = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i$ has variance $d_k$. So as $d_k$ grows, the raw scores grow with it. Plug very large logits into a softmax and you get a near-one-hot distribution — gradients vanish in the off-peaks and learning slows. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps the scores' standard deviation around $1$ regardless of $d_k$, so softmax stays in a useful regime.

Masking (causal / padding)

Two common masks:

  • Causal mask — used in decoders (and language models): position $i$ may only attend to positions $\le i$. We achieve this by adding $-\infty$ to the scores for "future" positions before the softmax, which makes their weights zero.
  • Padding mask — when batching variable-length sequences padded to a fixed length: zero out attention to the padding tokens, so they don't contribute.

Masking is purely additive in the logits; gradients flow through the unmasked entries normally.

Multi-head attention

One attention operation is one "view" of the relationships. Different aspects of meaning often live in different subspaces — syntactic structure, topical content, coreference. Multi-head attention runs several attention operations in parallel, each in its own learned subspace, then concatenates their outputs:

$$ \mathrm{head}_i = \mathrm{Attn}\!\bigl(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_Q^{(i)},\ \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_K^{(i)},\ \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_V^{(i)}\bigr), $$ $$ \mathrm{MHA}(\mathbf{X}) = \bigl[\mathrm{head}_1; \mathrm{head}_2; \ldots; \mathrm{head}_H\bigr]\, \mathbf{W}_O. $$

Each head has its own $\mathbf{W}_Q, \mathbf{W}_K, \mathbf{W}_V$ projecting into a smaller dimension $d_k = d / H$. The final $\mathbf{W}_O$ mixes the concatenated heads back to the original dimension $d$. Total parameters are similar to a single big-headed attention, but the model can attend to multiple things at once.

A walkthrough of a single head

Suppose we have three tokens $\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, \mathbf{x}_3$, each in $\mathbb{R}^4$, stacked as rows of $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 4}$. Let $\mathbf{W}_Q, \mathbf{W}_K, \mathbf{W}_V \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 2}$. Compute:

  1. $\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_Q \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}$. Three queries, each two-dimensional.
  2. $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_K \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}$. Three keys.
  3. $\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_V \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}$. Three values.
  4. $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top / \sqrt{2} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$. The score matrix; entry $S_{ij}$ is "how much should query $i$ attend to key $j$."
  5. $\mathbf{A} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$ — softmax row-wise. Each row sums to 1.
  6. $\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}$. Three new representations, each a learned mixture of the three values.

With three tokens we can draw $\mathbf{A}$ as a $3 \times 3$ heat map. A causal-masked self-attention has zeros above the diagonal: token 1 attends only to itself; token 2 attends to tokens 1 and 2; token 3 attends to all three.

Cost

Computing the full $n \times n$ score matrix is $\mathcal{O}(n^2 d)$ in time and memory. That's the headline drawback of transformers on very long sequences: a $32{,}000$-token document produces a billion-entry attention matrix per layer per head. A great deal of recent research (FlashAttention, sparse attention, linear attention, sliding-window attention) attacks this scaling.

Check yourself

  1. What are the three roles a token plays in self-attention?
  2. Why divide the dot products by $\sqrt{d_k}$?
  3. Why does the softmax in attention go along rows, not the whole matrix?
  4. What is a causal mask, and where is it used?
  5. What does multi-head attention buy you that a single big head doesn't?

Answers: query (what am I looking for), key (what do I match), and value (what I contribute when matched). To keep score variance from growing with $d_k$ and saturating the softmax. Each row is one query's distribution over keys; rows must independently sum to 1. A mask that zeroes attention to "future" positions, used in decoders / LMs to prevent peeking ahead. Multiple heads can attend to multiple kinds of relationships in parallel; one big head has to cram everything into one similarity space.
