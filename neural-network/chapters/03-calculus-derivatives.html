Derivatives

The slope of a curve at a point. The single most important idea in machine learning, because it tells us which way to nudge a parameter to make a loss go down.

The one-line motivation

We are eventually going to have a function called the loss, $L(w)$, that measures how wrong our network is when its weights are $w$. We want $L(w)$ to be small. If we know the slope of $L$ at the current $w$, we know which way "downhill" is. That's what a derivative gives us. Everything else in this chapter is technique for computing slopes accurately.

Slope of a line

For a straight line $y = mx + b$, the slope is $m$: the rise over the run. Move one unit right, go $m$ units up. That's it. The slope is the same everywhere on the line.

For a curve, the slope changes from point to point. We need a definition that lets us pin it down at one specific input.

The limit definition

Pick a function $f$ and an input $x$. Pick a small number $h$. The line through $\bigl(x, f(x)\bigr)$ and $\bigl(x+h, f(x+h)\bigr)$ — the secant line — has slope

$$ \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}. $$

Now imagine shrinking $h$ toward zero. The secant line tilts and lines up with the curve right at $x$ — the tangent line. The number it converges to (when it does) is the derivative of $f$ at $x$:

$$ f'(x) \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}. $$

We will not labor over the formal $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition of a limit. The intuition — "shrink $h$ and see what number the secant slope is heading toward" — is enough to derive every derivative we need.

One derivative from scratch

Let $f(x) = x^2$. Compute the derivative directly:

$$ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} (2x + h) \\ &= 2x. \end{aligned} $$

So the slope of $y = x^2$ at any point $x$ is $2x$. At $x=0$ the curve is flat. At $x=3$ it climbs at slope $6$. At $x=-1$ it descends at slope $-2$. That single result — derivative of $x^2$ is $2x$ — agrees with the visual: the parabola is steeper the farther you are from the vertex.

The rules of differentiation

Computing every derivative from the limit definition would be exhausting. Fortunately, a handful of rules let you build complicated derivatives from a small library of simple ones.

Power rule

$$ \frac{d}{dx} x^n \;=\; n\, x^{n-1}. $$

Examples: $\frac{d}{dx} x^3 = 3x^2$; $\frac{d}{dx} x^{1/2} = \tfrac{1}{2} x^{-1/2}$; $\frac{d}{dx} x = 1$; $\frac{d}{dx} 7 = 0$ (a constant has no slope).

Constant multiple and sum rules

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[c\,f(x)\bigr] = c\, f'(x), \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[f(x) + g(x)\bigr] = f'(x) + g'(x). $$

Constants pass through unchanged; sums differentiate term by term.

Product rule

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[f(x)\, g(x)\bigr] = f'(x)\, g(x) + f(x)\, g'(x). $$

Two-step memory aid: differentiate the first, leave the second alone, then add the first-times-derivative-of-second.

Quotient rule

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\!\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{f'(x)\, g(x) - f(x)\, g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2}. $$

Used less often than the product rule. You can usually rewrite a quotient as a product with a negative power and skip it.

Derivatives of $e^x$ and $\ln(x)$

Two facts so important we will use them again and again:

$$ \frac{d}{dx} e^x \;=\; e^x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx} \ln(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{x}. $$

The first one is the defining property of $e$: it is the unique base for which the exponential function equals its own derivative. That is exactly what makes $e$ special. (Try the same calculation with $2^x$ and you'll get a clumsier answer involving $\ln 2$.)

The second is the inverse of the first. We will derive it from the chain rule below as a sanity check.

The chain rule

This is the rule everything in this site eventually depends on. Suppose $y = f(g(x))$ — that is, $y$ is computed by first applying $g$ and then applying $f$. Let $u = g(x)$ for a moment, so $y = f(u)$. Then

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} \;=\; f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr) \cdot g'(x). $$

In words: the derivative of a composition is the product of derivatives of the pieces, evaluated at the right places.

Intuition: rates multiply along the chain

The chain rule is easier to remember as a sentence about rates than as a formula. Imagine three quantities linked in order: $x \to u \to y$. Suppose

  • when $x$ changes by a small amount, $u$ changes $3\times$ as fast — that's $\dfrac{du}{dx} = 3$;
  • when $u$ changes by a small amount, $y$ changes $5\times$ as fast — that's $\dfrac{dy}{du} = 5$.

How fast does $y$ change when $x$ changes? A nudge of size $\Delta x$ in $x$ produces a nudge of $3\,\Delta x$ in $u$, which then produces a nudge of $5 \cdot (3\,\Delta x) = 15\,\Delta x$ in $y$. So $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 15 = 5 \times 3$. That is the chain rule — rates multiply along the chain.

And it is also why the rule is multiplication, not addition: each link in the chain amplifies the previous link's nudge by its own local rate. Two amplifiers in series multiply their gains; they do not add.

Why this matters for backprop

A neural network is dozens or hundreds of compositions stacked on top of each other. To find how a small change in a weight at the bottom affects the loss at the top, you multiply derivatives along the chain. Backpropagation in Chapter 9 is exactly the chain rule, applied in a careful order — once for every layer, with the local rate at each layer being a derivative we already know how to compute.

Worked examples

Example 1. $y = (3x+1)^5$. Let $u = 3x+1$, $y = u^5$. Then $\dfrac{dy}{du} = 5u^4$ and $\dfrac{du}{dx} = 3$. Multiply:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = 5(3x+1)^4 \cdot 3 = 15(3x+1)^4. $$

Example 2. $y = e^{2x}$. Let $u = 2x$. Then $\dfrac{dy}{du} = e^u$, $\dfrac{du}{dx} = 2$, so $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 2 e^{2x}$.

Example 3 (the derivative of $\ln x$). Let $y = \ln x$, so $e^y = x$. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$. Using the chain rule on the left,

$$ e^y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 1, \qquad \text{therefore} \qquad \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{e^y} = \frac{1}{x}. $$

Derivative of the sigmoid (a preview)

We will need this in Chapter 6. The sigmoid is $\sigma(x) = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$. Use the chain rule on $1/(1+e^{-x}) = (1+e^{-x})^{-1}$. With $u = 1 + e^{-x}$ we have $\dfrac{du}{dx} = -e^{-x}$, so

$$ \sigma'(x) = -1 \cdot (1+e^{-x})^{-2} \cdot (-e^{-x}) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1+e^{-x})^2}. $$

Two short algebra steps later — multiply numerator and denominator by appropriate things — and we land on the famous tidy form:

$$ \sigma'(x) \;=\; \sigma(x)\bigl(1 - \sigma(x)\bigr). $$

That is the entire reason the sigmoid was popular for decades: its derivative can be computed from its output value alone, with no reuse of $x$. Worth remembering.

Higher derivatives, briefly

The derivative of the derivative is the second derivative, written $f''(x)$ or $\frac{d^2 y}{dx^2}$. It tells you how the slope itself is changing. We won't lean on second derivatives in this site — first-order gradient descent is enough — but it's worth knowing the notation exists, because some optimizers (e.g. second-order methods like Newton's) use it.

  2. What is $\frac{d}{dx} e^{-x}$?
  3. If $h(x) = \ln(x^2 + 1)$, what is $h'(x)$?
  4. What is $\sigma'(0)$, where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid?
  5. Why does the chain rule have a multiplication, not an addition?

Answers: $4x^3 - 3$; $-e^{-x}$; $\dfrac{2x}{x^2+1}$ (chain rule with inner $u=x^2+1$); $\sigma(0) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so $\sigma'(0) = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{4}$; because composing functions multiplies their effects: a small change $\Delta x$ produces $g'(x)\Delta x$ in $u$, which then produces $f'(u) g'(x)\Delta x$ in the output.
