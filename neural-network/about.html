About

How this site works

This is a self-paced reading path that takes you from high-school algebra to the Transformer block, with no steps skipped. Every chapter is a single page; every page is plain HTML, math, and hand-drawn SVG diagrams. There is no login, no tracking, no server. Your reading progress lives entirely in your browser's localStorage.

What you need to know already

Almost nothing.

  • High-school algebra. You should be comfortable with variables, exponents, and reading a function like $f(x) = 2x + 1$.
  • Basic graph reading. If you can read a chart with an $x$-axis and a $y$-axis, you are ready.
  • That's it. No calculus, no linear algebra, no programming. Chapters 1–5 build all of those from the ground up before you ever see a neuron.

How to read it

Read the chapters in order. The roadmap on the home page shows the dependency graph — every chapter is built from the ones that point to it, and assumes nothing else.

Each chapter is sized for a focused 30–60 minute session. Don't rush. The math is not hard, but it is dense; one careful read beats three skims.

Every chapter ends with a "Self-check" section. If you can answer those questions in your own words, the chapter has done its job. If you can't, scroll back up — the answer is somewhere on the page, not in your memory.

How progress works

  • A chapter flips to in progress the moment you start scrolling through it.
  • It flips to complete when the page's "Chapter complete" footer scrolls into view — that is, when you've read to the end.
  • Downstream chapters become ready (highlighted) once all their prerequisites are complete.
  • State is stored in your browser only. Clearing site data, or clicking Reset progress in the footer, wipes it.
  • Different browsers and devices each track their own progress.

Why the chapters are ordered the way they are

The site is divided into three movements:

  1. The mathematical foundations (Ch 1–5). Numbers, vectors, derivatives, gradients, probability. Nothing here mentions neural networks. These five chapters are the alphabet; the rest of the site is the writing.
  2. The neural network proper (Ch 6–10). Single neuron, loss, gradient descent, multi-layer perceptron, backpropagation, training tricks. By the end of Chapter 10 you can describe — equations included — exactly what happens in one training step of a deep network.
  3. Architectures (Ch 11–14). Convolutions for images. Recurrence for sequences. Attention to fix recurrence. Transformers as the synthesis. Each architecture answers a specific failure of the previous one.

You can use the dependency graph to skip around if you already know parts of the early material. The graph is honest: if a chapter doesn't point to it, it really isn't required. But every chapter from 6 onward depends transitively on Chapters 2–4, so the math foundations are not optional.

Editorial choices

  • Math first, code never. Not a single line of Python. The only way to truly understand a neural network is the math; the code is a thin shadow of it. You can implement it in any language once you have the equations.
  • Derivations, not appeals to authority. Whenever a formula appears, the chapter shows where it came from. The "why" is more important than the "what."
  • One worked example per concept. Every chapter that introduces an algorithm walks through it on small numbers you can verify with a calculator.
  • No interactive demos. Sliders and live training animations are fun but easy to use as a substitute for understanding. This site is for reading and thinking.
  • Plain words. Where a piece of jargon is unavoidable, the chapter introduces it explicitly. Where it is avoidable, it is avoided.

Offline reading

The only network call any page makes is to load KaTeX from a public CDN for math typesetting. Save the site to disk and the chapters work offline; equations will fall back to plain TeX source if the CDN is unreachable. Everything else — text, diagrams, progress tracking, the roadmap — is local.

If something is wrong

The site is one author's attempt to explain a hard subject clearly from first principles. There will be places where the explanation could be sharper, a derivation could be tighter, or a diagram could be clearer. If you spot one, your time would be well spent re-explaining the idea to yourself in your own words — that is, after all, the only useful way to read mathematics.
